# D138: Biomedical Engineering — specialization audit

Status: `source_reviewed_candidate_structure_six_practice_families`. This is a source-grounded candidate review, not canonical taxonomy approval or academic-body sign-off.

## Existing structure

Current subgroup-record count: **5**. Current records: Biomedical Systems & Computational Engineering (7 roles); Health professionals (4 roles); Biomedical Engineering Technicians (1 role); Biomedical Laboratory Support (1 role); Assemblers (1 role)

Five records mix a broad computational/system label, health-profession occupation aggregate, biomedical technician/support job families and assembly. They do not provide a coherent map of biomedical engineering specializations.

## Scope and proposed disposition

Parent boundary: Retain biomedical engineering as engineering of biological/medical systems, devices and technologies across research, development, evaluation and lifecycle use. Separate clinical engineering/health-technology management from device design where learning goals differ, while allowing explicitly supported connections. Clinical care roles and generic assembly are not automatically biomedical engineering specializations.

WHO describes biomedical engineers' roles across the medical-device lifecycle, while BMES professional meeting tracks identify established areas including biomaterials, biomechanics, imaging/instrumentation, neural engineering, rehabilitation, tissue engineering, biomedical robotics and computational/systems biology. Six candidate groupings provide a tractable navigation structure, not a fixed global standard. The current five groups are materially miscategorized and should be replaced only after role-by-role reconciliation.

## Candidate areas of specialization

- **Medical Devices and Clinical Engineering** — Design, evaluation, selection, safe deployment, maintenance and lifecycle management of medical equipment and health technologies. Boundary: WHO identifies lifecycle work from conception to use and health-technology management; distinguish device engineering from clinical operation.
- **Biomedical Imaging and Instrumentation** — Sensors, measurement systems, biomedical instrumentation and technologies that acquire or process biological/clinical signals and images. Boundary: Medical imaging physics is a closely related discipline; avoid duplicate clinical-physics ownership without a clear engineering-versus-physics distinction.
- **Biomaterials, Tissue and Cellular Engineering** — Materials and engineered biological systems for tissue interaction, repair, manufacturing or therapeutic delivery. Boundary: Keep cell/tissue engineering and biomaterials related but not assume every drug-delivery pathway belongs here.
- **Biomechanics, Rehabilitation and Assistive Technology** — Mechanical analysis and engineering of the body, movement, orthoses/prostheses, rehabilitation and assistive systems. Boundary: Clinical rehabilitation professions remain separate; this is the engineering design/evaluation route.
- **Neural and Bioelectrical Engineering** — Engineering of neural interfaces, neurotechnology, bioelectrical signals and related stimulation/recording systems. Boundary: A distinct, established research/practice area; do not collapse into generic instrumentation if it has a distinct learner pathway.
- **Computational, Systems and Quantitative Bioengineering** — Computational and systems approaches to biological/physiological systems, biomedical data and engineering models. Boundary: Methods may cross every other branch; present as a specialization only when the learner chooses this deeper pathway, not as shared foundation by default.

## Boundary audit

- Omissions / over-splitting: Do not turn every application sector (cancer, cardiovascular, orthopaedic, infectious disease) into a separate top-level specialization; some are application contexts spanning methods. Do not absorb nurses, physicians, laboratory aides or production assemblers solely because they use or build medical technology.
- Computational signals: Computational/systems biology, image analysis, modeling, signal processing and device-control methods are explicit parts of the field. Computation is cross-cutting; keep computational biology distinct where the core work is biological data/modeling rather than engineering a biomedical technology.
- Jurisdiction note: Medical-device regulation, clinical-engineering scope, protected titles and approval pathways differ by jurisdiction. WHO's lifecycle account is international guidance and emphasizes context-specific technology assessment.

## Evidence and limits

- [World Health Organization (WHO): Human resources for medical devices: the role of biomedical engineers](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789241565479) — WHO Medical Device Technical Series (2022), 240-page report: biomedical-engineer roles across medical-device conception, design/development, regulation, evaluation, training and use/lifecycle. Supports: Authoritative global framing for the parent scope and the clinical-engineering/device-lifecycle route. Limitation: WHO document describes role breadth, not a mandated count or naming scheme for specializations.
- [Biomedical Engineering Society (BMES): Tracks and Subtracks](https://member.bmes.org/tracks-and-subtracks) — Lists recurring technical areas including biomaterials, biomechanics, biomedical imaging/instrumentation, neural engineering, orthopaedic/rehabilitation engineering, tissue engineering, device technologies/biomedical robotics, and bioinformatics/computational/systems biology. Supports: Provides a broad professional-community cross-check that the candidate subject areas are established and diverse. Limitation: Conference tracks are a US-based society's research/meeting organization, not a globally binding or exhaustive taxonomy.
- [WHO: Health technology management](https://cdn.who.int/media/docs/default-source/medical-devices/health-technology-management/country-data-on-health-technology-management.pdf?sfvrsn=4c8eefbb_3) — Defines health-technology management/clinical engineering work such as needs assessment, selection, procurement, inventory, installation, maintenance, safe-use training and decommissioning. Supports: Supports distinguishing lifecycle/clinical-engineering practice from design-only and generic assembly buckets. Limitation: Operational healthcare-system organization varies by country and health system.

Sources support discipline and practice distinctions; they do not by themselves establish a universal number of student-facing areas. Current occupational-group membership is not used as proof of domain/subgroup correctness. No role-to-specialization mapping is made.
