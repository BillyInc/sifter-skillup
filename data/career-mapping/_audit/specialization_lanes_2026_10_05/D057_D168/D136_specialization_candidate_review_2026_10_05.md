# D136: Aeronautical and Aerospace Engineering — specialization audit

Status: `source_reviewed_two_routes_direct_curriculum_distinction`. This is a source-grounded candidate review, not canonical taxonomy approval or academic-body sign-off.

## Existing structure

Current subgroup-record count: **7**. Current records: Aircraft & Aerospace Engineering (4 roles); Aircraft Assembly & Inspection (7 roles); Numerical and material recording clerks (2 roles); Metal, machinery and related trades workers (3 roles); Food processing, wood working, garment and other craft and related trades workers (1 role); Assemblers (2 roles); Drivers and mobile plant operators (1 role)

Seven records mix engineering disciplines with clerical, manufacturing/trade, assembly and mobile-plant occupation families. These are not seven aerospace areas of specialization.

## Scope and proposed disposition

Parent boundary: The combined parent explicitly spans aeronautical (atmospheric flight) and astronautical (spaceflight) engineering. Keep both routes visible beneath a shared engineering foundation; generic manufacturing/technician work may be supporting occupational paths but is not itself a specialty of engineering without distinct training evidence.

Two candidate routes are unusually well supported: ABET expressly distinguishes aeronautical and astronautical topic sets and says programs titled aerospace combine them while ensuring depth in one and coverage in the other. This is an accreditation curriculum distinction, not a universal rule that every aerospace career must select exactly one branch. The seven current occupational-group subgroups should not be used as the student specialization set.

## Candidate areas of specialization

- **Aeronautical Engineering and Atmospheric Flight Systems** — Design, analysis and testing of aircraft and atmospheric flight systems, including aerodynamics, propulsion, structures/materials, stability/control and flight systems. Boundary: Atmospheric aircraft are the defining scope; aviation operations and maintenance licensing are separate contextual routes.
- **Astronautical Engineering and Spaceflight Systems** — Design and integration of spacecraft, launch/spaceflight systems, orbital environments, mission systems and relevant propulsion/communications/control subsystems. Boundary: Spaceflight vehicle/system engineering; scientific study of celestial phenomena belongs to D132.

## Boundary audit

- Omissions / over-splitting: Do not split propulsion, structures, avionics, flight dynamics, launch vehicles and spacecraft platforms into unrelated top-level areas by default; these are deeper engineering tracks within or across the two supported routes. Keep aerospace science (D132), pilot/air-traffic occupations, and aircraft maintenance authorization distinct.
- Computational signals: CFD, finite-element analysis, flight/mission simulation, optimization, autonomy and digital engineering are cross-cutting tools/methods across both routes; they do not independently prove a computational specialization or role.
- Jurisdiction note: Aircraft airworthiness, maintenance authorization, export controls, licensing and space-launch/mission rules vary by state and regulator. ABET's program criteria are US accreditation criteria with international reach, not a global regulator.
- No-specialization disposition: Not applicable: evidence supports candidate area(s), subject to the stated residuals.

## Evidence and limits

- [ABET / American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics: Criteria for Accrediting Engineering Programs, 2025–2026](https://www.abet.org/accreditation/accreditation-criteria/criteria-for-accrediting-engineering-programs-2025-2026/) — Lines 320–331: aerospace criteria cover aerospace/aeronautical/astronautical titles; aeronautical and astronautical criteria differ, and aerospace programs combine both with specified depth. Supports: Directly supports the two candidate branches and the shared combined parent. Limit: ABET is an accreditation body; the criterion describes program content and does not prescribe a global career-path taxonomy.

Sources support discipline and practice distinctions; they do not by themselves establish a universal number of student-facing areas. Current occupational-group membership is not used as proof of domain/subgroup correctness. No role-to-specialization mapping is made.
