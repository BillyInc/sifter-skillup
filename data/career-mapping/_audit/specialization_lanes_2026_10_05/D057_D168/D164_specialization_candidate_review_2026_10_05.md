# D164: Urban Mobility and Smart City Systems — specialization audit

Status: `source_reviewed_custom_group_partly_relevant_parent_smart_city_scope_needs_boundary`. Evidence review only; not canonical taxonomy approval or academic-body sign-off.

## Existing structure

Current subgroup-record count: **5**. Current records: Production and specialised services managers (1); Smart Mobility & Urban Systems (12); Teaching professionals (1); Science and engineering associate professionals (2); Numerical and material recording clerks (2)

One custom mobility systems group is surrounded by unrelated generic occupation families. The parent combines urban transport planning/operations with citywide digital systems and governance; not all smart-city systems are mobility, and mobility is not all digital.

## Parent scope and disposition

Retain mobility as the anchor. Treat urban mobility planning, public transport/service operations and transport-system engineering/data as coherent branches; include smart-city integration only where it is mobility-relevant. The title may overstate citywide smart-systems scope.

UN-Habitat's materials describe sustainable urban mobility as integrated with accessible urban planning, public transport and active mobility, while its Smart City Outlook lists mobility applications such as real-time traffic management, low-emission vehicles, mobility-as-a-service, shared mobility and active mobility. This supports four candidate work areas but not a universal four-subgroup mandate. Current four generic groups do not fit; the custom mobility group is directionally relevant but overly broad. Smart-city breadth must be bounded to mobility.

## Candidate areas of specialization

- **Sustainable Urban Mobility Planning and Policy** — Plan accessible, safe, low-impact urban movement and integrate land use, public transport and active mobility. Boundary: UN-Habitat situates mobility planning within sustainable urban development; this is planning/policy work, not vehicle engineering.
- **Public Transport and Shared-Mobility Systems** — Plan, manage and improve public transport networks, services, interchanges and shared mobility operations. Boundary: Transit operators, authorities and service platforms have distinct operational responsibilities.
- **Transport Technology, ITS and Mobility Data** — Use sensing, traffic management, mobility-as-a-service, data systems and transport engineering to operate or improve mobility networks. Boundary: Technical systems skills are specific to these roles; digitalization is not a requirement for every urban planner or transit professional.
- **Smart-City Mobility Integration and Governance** — Coordinate mobility systems with urban digital infrastructure, public services, standards, privacy and city governance. Boundary: Retain only mobility-facing smart-city work; city energy, health or utilities belong to their own domains.

## Boundaries and cross-cutting considerations

- Omissions / over-splitting: Do not create a subgroup for each travel mode, vehicle type, software platform, city or planning policy. Keep civil/transport engineering, urban planning, transit operations and digital systems linked but distinct when the learning requirements diverge.
- Computational signal: Transport modeling, GIS, traffic operations, sensor data, route/network optimization and real-time systems support selected branches; no single toolset is shared across planning, operations and engineering.
- Jurisdiction note: Planning powers, transit regulation, data/privacy rules, accessibility requirements, procurement and infrastructure standards are local or national. UN-Habitat guidance is global; case studies and local policies must be labeled by place.

## Sources and limits

- [UN-Habitat: Planning and Design for Sustainable Urban Mobility: Global Report on Human Settlements](https://unhabitat.org/planning-and-design-for-sustainable-urban-mobility-global-report-on-human-settlements-2013) — Report frames mobility through integrated urban planning and accessibility, not simply expansion of transport infrastructure. Supports: Global planning scope and need to distinguish urban mobility planning from engineering or smart-city technology alone. Limitation: Policy report does not prescribe exact professional specialization labels.
- [UN-Habitat: UN-Habitat Smart Cities Outlook, section 6.3 Mobility](https://unhabitat.org/sites/default/files/2024/12/un_smart_city_outlook.pdf) — Section 6.3 describes real-time traffic management, low-emission vehicles, mobility-as-a-service, shared mobility and active-mobility programmes. Supports: Mobility technology/data use cases and bounded smart-city mobility integration. Limitation: Technology examples are not evidence that every mobility learner needs these tools or that smart cities form a separate universal profession.

The sources support review candidates and boundaries, not a universal required subgroup count. Existing role membership is not treated as evidence of fit. No role-to-specialization mapping is made.
