### What the domain studies

**Social Sciences & Behavioural Research studies how people live together, how groups and institutions work, and how social conditions shape people’s lives. Depending on the question, researchers may use numbers, people’s accounts, existing records, or a mix of these to build and explain evidence.**

“Quantitative” means working with information expressed as numbers—for example, counting how many people use a service. “Qualitative” means studying information such as people’s words, observations or documents to understand meaning and experience. A survey can help compare answers across a group; interviews can help explain how people understand or experience an issue. These are options, not methods every learner must use.

This is a broad umbrella, not one discipline, one job, one method or one fixed entry route.

**Staff evidence note—not learner copy:** UNESCO’s education-field classification places sociology, psychology, economics and political science within social and behavioural sciences, while journalism is grouped separately under the broader field of social sciences, journalism and information. UNESCO’s R&D statistics grouping draws some boundaries differently. These are classification systems for different purposes; neither automatically decides Sifter’s D078 boundary. Reconcile the final wording with D078’s approved definition before release. See the source locators for [UNESCO’s social-science introduction](https://www.unesco.org/en/query-list/s/social-science-research), [ISCED detailed field descriptions](https://www.uis.unesco.org/sites/default/files/medias/fichiers/2025/04/international-standard-classification-of-education-fields-of-education-and-training-2013-detailed-field-descriptions-2015-en.pdf), [ISCED-F codes](https://uis.unesco.org/sites/default/files/documents/isced-fields-of-education-and-training-2013-en.pdf), and [UNESCO’s R&D classification manual](https://uis.unesco.org/sites/default/files/documents/uis_rd_m_2016_en_0.pdf).

### What the work can look like

A research team might turn a question into a study plan, gather information through a survey, interviews, observation or existing records, check what the information can and cannot show, and share the result in a report, presentation or policy brief. Depending on the project, researchers may work with other researchers, educators, public officials, community organisations and the people whose experiences or records inform the study.

### Opportunities and constraints

Social research can help an organisation understand a problem and consider what to do next. Findings still depend on the question, who or what was studied, how information was gathered and what the evidence supports. A survey of one group does not automatically describe everyone, and a study does not automatically prove that a policy will work everywhere.

## Proposed domain learning path (research candidates only)

The domain layer should orient the learner to the shared way of approaching social questions, not promise employment in every part of the field. The evidence does **not** show that all techniques, software or advanced subject knowledge are shared by every area. Therefore, most items below are labelled “useful” or “optional”; “needed” is reserved for a responsibility that applies when the learner actually undertakes research involving people or sensitive data. The JSON ledger is a review queue: it records candidate locators and explanations, but many claims, relationships, checkpoint fits and learning destinations are still unverified or missing. No record is student-ready merely because it has a URL.

1. **Start with the social question.** Ask what part of people’s lives, relationships or institutions you want to understand. Keep the question clear and narrow enough to investigate. This is an orientation, not a universal job prerequisite.
2. **Plan what evidence could answer it.** Research design is the plan that connects a question to the information and method that could answer it. A survey may show how common something is in a suitable sample; interviews may help explain people’s experiences. Choose based on the question rather than trying to use every method.
3. **Read evidence carefully.** Check where information came from, who or what was included, and what the results can and cannot show. Quantitative evidence can help count or compare; qualitative evidence can help explain meaning or experience. Neither automatically replaces the other.
4. **Work responsibly.** When research involves people or sensitive/identifiable information, think about consent, privacy, possible harm and the review or permission rules that apply. Ethical responsibility continues across the work; selecting an item does not grant permission to conduct research.
5. **Explain the result and its limits.** Share findings in a format that fits the audience and purpose. Say how you reached the conclusion and what should not be concluded from the evidence.

The sequence is a proposed teaching route, not a universal chronology or a qualification standard. Quantitative and qualitative methods may be alternatives or justified complements; neither is automatically a prerequisite for the other. A tool is selected only when it fits a chosen method and task.

The route should be displayed as a short explanation followed by the two
foundation bands above, then optional route cards. A learner should first see
what is shared, then choose which kind of work they want to explore. The UI
must not render the 19-item audit queue as a flat list, and it must not silently
turn alternatives into cumulative requirements.

### Named tools and libraries: route choices, not domain requirements

Named software belongs after the learner understands the task or method it
supports. It should not appear in the shared foundation as if every D078
learner needs it. The eventual route cards should explain the purpose in plain
language, show alternatives, state what the tool cannot replace, and provide a
verified learning link:

- **Quantitative analysis:** SPSS, R, Python or Jamovi can help organise data,
  calculate summaries or run selected analyses. The learner still needs a
  suitable question, design, measurement and statistical judgement.
- **Python data workflow:** pandas can clean and reshape tables; statsmodels
  can fit statistical models; Matplotlib can make charts; and Jupyter can keep
  code, explanations and results together in a reproducible notebook. These
  are connected choices, not a checklist that every learner must complete.
- **Qualitative research:** NVivo can help organise and code substantial
  interviews, documents or open-ended responses. Manual coding, Taguette,
  MAXQDA and ATLAS.ti are possible alternatives; the software does not choose
  the research question or interpret the evidence for the learner.
- **Relationship-data analysis:** NetworkX or Gephi can represent and analyse
  relationships as a network. A graph does not by itself prove causality or
  explain the social meaning of a relationship.
- **Agent-based modelling:** Mesa can build and analyse simulated social
  systems in Python. Casevo is an emerging specialist option. A simulation
  helps explore assumptions; its output is not automatically evidence of how
  real people or communities behave.
- **Predictive or text modelling:** scikit-learn is a conditional specialist
  option for a computational and digital social-research route. It may support
  prediction or text-feature modelling only when the question, evaluation
  plan, and safeguards for bias and interpretation are defined. It is not a
  shared foundation tool and prediction does not establish explanation,
  causality, fairness or social validity.

The local inventory and deep-search checkpoint support these as conditional,
specialist or alternative route choices, not as universal requirements. Their

## Current inventory cautions

The local D078 structure contains role-derived skill prevalence and two existing subgroup records. Those frequencies are an inventory signal only: a skill appearing in many role records does not prove that it is genuinely shared across the domain. In particular, academic/scientific writing, research methodology, research-data management, statistics, named software and subject knowledge require checkpoint-specific validation before display. This review does not alter the records or audit roles.

The most defensible preliminary framing is not “everyone must learn all of these.” It is: **start with a clear social question and careful use of evidence; then choose the methods, knowledge and tools that fit the learner’s selected direction.** The programme overview and pre-domain-entry explanation must tell the learner what the field is and why the programme connects before this path is shown.
