# D078 Student-Facing Content Clarity Standard

**Prepared:** 2026-11-03  
**Purpose:** A reusable editorial and display contract for explaining every student-facing D078 programme-to-role concept accurately, accessibly and in context.  
**Applies to:** Programme and domain overviews; foundations; methods and named designs; areas; roles; learning items; relationship explanations; tools; resources; credentials; standards; prompts, labels, instructions and report text.  
**Does not authorize:** Changing graph ownership, requirements, route logic, sequence, taxonomy, role fit, evidence claims, or student approval. Copy work must preserve those decisions and request an evidence-owner review when they are unclear.

## 1. Editorial standard

Write for a mixed audience: a student encountering the subject for the first time, an adult changing direction, and a reader who already knows the field. Do not assume a university-level vocabulary, but do not hide the real concepts behind vague wording.

The central rule is:

> **Keep the accurate professional term. Explain its meaning and operation in the same flow, then connect it to a concrete example, the learner's context, and its limits.**

The first explanation must be understandable without a dictionary, a tooltip, or prior specialist knowledge. A glossary, link, or expanded card can add depth; it cannot carry the only explanation of a term needed to understand the main text.

This operationalizes the intended-reader principle in [ISO 24495-1:2023, Plain language — Part 1](https://committee.iso.org/standard/78907.html): information should let intended readers find what they need, understand what they find and use it. ISO describes plain language as applicable to technical writing as well as general-public material. This is a writing and review standard, not an ISO certification claim.

The same rule is reflected in the [UK Department for Education's plain-language guidance](https://design.education.gov.uk/content-design/plain-language): when technical terms are needed, provide a plain-language description on first use. The [U.S. Federal Plain Language Guidelines](https://www.eac.gov/sites/default/files/eac_assets/1/6/FederalPLGuidelines.pdf) say not to remove necessary technical terms; define them when they cannot be avoided, and keep the surrounding language clear. The [CDC Clear Communication Index](https://www.cdc.gov/ccindex/) adds a research-based review approach; its supporting [user guide](https://www.cdc.gov/ccindex/pdf/clear-communication-user-guide.pdf) cautions that readability scores alone cannot establish comprehension. For digital structure and instructions, apply [WCAG 2.2 guidance on labels and instructions](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/labels-or-instructions/) and [headings and labels](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/headings-and-labels.html).

## 2. The explanation sequence

For a technical concept, guide the learner through these questions in this order. Not every card needs a separate heading for every question, but the explanation must answer the relevant ones.

1. **What is it?** Give the correct professional name and a direct definition.
2. **What does it do?** Explain the operation or reasoning, not merely the fact that a computer, tool or method is involved.
3. **How does it work, at this level?** Name the inputs, main action and output. Use a small example, analogy or diagram where it improves understanding; identify the analogy's limits.
4. **Why is it here?** Connect the concept to this programme, D078, selected method/design, area, role task or learner choice. Never make the reader infer the connection.
5. **What comes next?** Explain what this item builds on, what it prepares the learner to do, and why the next item follows. Do not invent a dependency to make the sequence look tidy.
6. **What does it not establish?** State the important boundary: assumptions, uncertainty, data limitations, ethical obligations, jurisdiction, qualification or maturity, as applicable.

The main overview should carry the first four points and the most important limit. Details can be progressive: short overview first, then clearly labelled explanation, worked example, assumptions/limits, related choices, and resources. Do not repeat the same definition in the preview and expanded card.

## 3. How to preserve technical depth while explaining terms

Use the **term → operation → example → interpretation** pattern:

- **Term:** use the disciplinary term learners will meet in courses and work.
- **Operation:** explain what it actually does in ordinary verbs: classify, compare, estimate, represent, simulate, sample, measure, interpret, validate.
- **Example:** show a plausible input and output in a real social-science context.
- **Interpretation:** say what the output supports and what it cannot establish by itself.

Avoid two opposite failures:

- **Jargon stack:** “Apply computational methods to large-scale digital traces for inference.” The reader must decode several terms before learning what happens.
- **Watered-down label:** “Use computers to study people.” This drops the technical object, method, reasoning and limits.

Prefer a professional term followed immediately by a useful explanation. For example: “A network model represents people or organizations as points and their relationships as links, so a researcher can measure how a group is connected.” Keep terms such as *network model*, *algorithm*, *statistical model*, *agent-based model*, *measurement*, *validity* and *causal inference* when they are the subject being taught; explain each precisely at first use. Do not explain a specialist term by replacing it with another unexplained specialist term.

### Technical-term test

For every term that a non-specialist may not know, ask:

- Does the sentence tell the reader what the term means here?
- Does it explain what the method or object does, rather than merely provide a synonym?
- Does a concrete example show the input, action or reasoning, and result?
- Could a reader mistake a pattern, estimate, model output or simulation for proof about the real world?
- Is the technical detail correct according to the evidence owner and appropriate for this learning level?

If the answer to any of the first three is no, improve the explanation. If the fourth is yes, add the boundary. If the fifth is uncertain, do not simplify by guessing; ask the item/evidence owner.

### Mandatory technical-term sweep: no assumed vocabulary

Before copy can pass review, perform a **term-by-term sweep of every student-facing text unit**, not a spot check of the main definition. Include titles, subtitles, overview paragraphs, examples, captions, diagrams, button/helper text, relationship explanations, resource rationales, role tasks and report text. Examples are not exempt: they often introduce new specialist vocabulary.

For each text unit:

1. List every domain-specific term, acronym, compressed noun phrase, method name, statistic, data type, platform term, legal/credential term, and phrase that a first-time reader might need to look up. Include terms used inside examples and caveats (for example, *digital traces*, *administrative records*, *bias*, *validation*, *network*, *simulation* and *agent*).
2. Record each term in a **terminology audit ledger** with: exact term; plain-language explanation used in the copy; source or subject-matter basis; first-use location; whether this screen can be entered directly or viewed independently; and reviewer result.
3. Check that the explanation comes immediately with the term, tells what it means **in this context**, and does not depend on another unexplained term. If the explanation introduces another specialist term, add that term to the ledger and define it too. Repeat until the explanation chain reaches language a novice can understand.
4. Check later uses for consistent meaning. Re-explain at first use on a separate screen, card, report or route when a learner can arrive there without seeing the earlier definition. Hidden or previously visited content cannot be assumed.
5. Have a reviewer who is not a subject-matter expert flag any word or phrase they would need to search for. Resolve every flag: define it inline, replace it with a precise familiar phrase, or retain the technical term with an immediate explanation. Do not dismiss a flag because the editor or expert already knows the term.
6. Have the subject-matter reviewer confirm the plain explanation has not changed the technical concept, scope, assumptions or strength of claim.

The copy fails this check if **any unexplained term remains**, including one term inside an example. A glossary may reinforce the explanation but cannot replace an inline first-use explanation. Automated acronym/readability checks help find candidates but do not complete this sweep.

## 4. D078-specific explanation contract

D078 is **Social Sciences & Behavioural Research**. The learning journey must explain social inquiry as work on questions about people, relationships, institutions and social processes, while preserving distinctions among research designs, methods, techniques, tools and areas. The Sociology programme is context for the learner's starting point; it is not evidence that every domain item was taught by that programme.

The current D078 journey has three compulsory shared foundations before method choice. Optional and conditional domain items, including computational social research, must be presented as optional when that is the graph disposition. Explain why an item is shown and what it can add without implying that it is required for every student, every area or every role. The graph and route contracts—not editorial preference—control requirements and downstream visibility.

For content above and below method choice:

- **Programme overview:** explain the actual programme themes and learning outcomes without duplicating them or presenting programme content as a domain requirement.
- **Programme-to-domain bridge:** say what prior learning is relevant and what the wider domain path adds. Do not claim equivalence, mastery, eligibility or qualification.
- **Domain overview:** explain the field, its questions, common work outputs, multiple approaches and the route's relation to programme context. It must orient someone who has not selected a method yet.
- **Overview entry gate:** every route into an area or role—including a direct link or restored saved route—must present the D078 domain overview first. Do not treat loaded graph data, a prior hidden screen or a valid route selection as proof that the learner saw it. After the overview step, return the learner to the requested destination.
- **Compulsory foundation:** explain why it applies across the defined route, what it prepares the learner to do, and any boundary. Do not imply it alone qualifies a learner for all areas or roles.
- **Method/design overview:** explain the kind of question it can address, the evidence it uses, the main reasoning/workflow, what the learner could produce, and when its assumptions or limits matter. Explain that alternatives depend on the question and evidence.
- **Area overview:** name the area’s actual subject boundary, typical problems and outputs, and what selected domain learning carries into it. Keep area-owned learning separate from domain-owned useful connections.
- **Role overview:** describe the specific task/context that makes the role relevant to the selected area. Conditional routes must say when they apply. A role title is not a promise of employment, professional eligibility or universal duties.
- **Item explanation:** explain the item's contribution in the selected method, area, role task or route. Do not concatenate internal route notes, audit status, tasks and guardrails into one paragraph.
- **Relationship explanation:** state plainly whether the next item is a prerequisite, a recommended next step, one alternative, a complement, a tool that implements a method, or an optional useful connection. Explain why the relation matters here.
- **Resource explanation:** say what the specific resource teaches and where to start. Do not imply that a link proves mastery or covers more than the reviewed locator supports.

### D078 computational social research: exemplar standard

The phrase **computational techniques** is not self-explanatory. Keep it only if the same explanation says what these techniques do. The learner should understand that this is a family of approaches—not one method, not a software package, and not simply the use of a computer.

The following is a **copy model for review**, not automatic approval of graph text. Verify its match to the current item, method routes, ownership, examples and evidence before using it:

> **Computational social research applies algorithms, statistical models and simulations to questions about society and social behaviour. An algorithm is a set of precise steps for processing or analyzing data; a statistical model estimates patterns or relationships; a simulation runs a defined model to explore what could happen under stated assumptions. Researchers can use these approaches with surveys, administrative records, documents, networks or digital traces—the records created when people use digital services, such as posts, comments or other recorded interactions. Those records may be private, incomplete or shaped by who uses a service, so researchers must check access rights, privacy and how well they represent the people being studied. For example, network analysis represents people or organizations as points connected by relationships, while computational text analysis applies defined procedures to find or classify patterns in a collection of documents. Agent-based modelling represents individuals as simulated agents following specified rules, then examines the group patterns that result. These approaches can help organize evidence, measure patterns or test scenarios. Their results depend on the research question, data quality, model assumptions and validation; a pattern does not by itself explain its cause or prove it applies to everyone. This is an optional route when the question and evidence call for computational analysis.**

Editorial checks on this model:

- “Algorithm,” “statistical model,” “simulation,” “digital traces,” “network analysis,” and “agent-based modelling” receive an explanation at first use.
- The definition does not say that all computational social research requires big data, social-media data, machine learning or programming at the same level.
- Examples show what is represented or processed; they do not imply automated interpretation of social meaning.
- Model outputs are not treated as causal proof or universal truth.
- Optionality follows the current requirement contract and is stated in learner language.
- If the overview is too long for its placement, preserve the first-sentence definition and core operation, then progressively disclose examples and limits. Do not delete the technical meaning to meet an arbitrary word count.

## 5. Content types and recommended structure

| Content type | Minimum useful explanation | Common failure to prevent |
|---|---|---|
| Overview | What this field/route studies; how work is done; representative outputs; why it matters to the learner; boundary or choice point. | Abstract catalogue of nouns, or a promotional claim about careers. |
| Definition | Accurate category plus distinguishing features and scope. | Circular definition (“computational methods are methods using computation”). |
| Method/design | Question fit; evidence/input; reasoning/process; output; assumptions/limits; alternatives or combinations. | Listing tools or techniques without explaining the research logic. |
| Technique/model | What is represented/calculated; input; result; assumptions; interpretation. | Calling a model result “the answer” or confusing prediction with explanation/causation. |
| Tool/software | What task the tool supports; data accepted; useful output; relevant constraints; alternatives if verified. | Treating a tool as a method, a requirement, or the only valid product. |
| Skill | Observable learner action and a realistic standard of performance. | Vague phrases such as “understands data” or “has analytical skills.” |
| Competency | A contextual ability combining knowledge and actions, with evidence of judgment and communication. | Inferring competence from having selected a card, used a tool, or earned a certificate. |
| Standard/regulation | Who issues it; what it applies to; jurisdiction/version/date; what it requires or recommends. | Making a local rule universal or showing a draft as binding. |
| Credential | Issuer, subject/scope, level, eligibility/cost/renewal when verified, and whether optional. | Implying universal recognition or job eligibility. |
| Relationship/sequence | Relation type, why it applies, prerequisite/output, next step, whether skippable. | An unlabeled arrow, arbitrary order, or optional edge that blocks progress. |
| Role/task context | Concrete task, setting/condition, relevant learning and output. | Broad job-title prose with no area/task link. |
| Resource | What this exact resource covers, locator/start point, format/access limits, and connection to selected item. | Placeholder button, unexplained link, or inflated coverage claim. |

## 6. Voice and sentence rules

- Be clear, specific, calm and professional. Respect both younger learners and adults; never use baby talk, forced slang, or condescending “simple” analogies.
- Address the learner directly where it helps. Explain the subject rather than selling it.
- Put the main point first. Use active verbs and one main idea per sentence where possible.
- Prefer familiar words around necessary technical terms. Avoid long noun chains, unexplained acronyms, vague verbs (“utilize,” “leverage,” “facilitate”) and promotional adjectives (“cutting-edge,” “revolutionary”) unless they are evidenced and relevant.
- Keep one idea per paragraph. Use descriptive headings and lists when they help readers scan; headings must still make sense without surrounding visual design.
- Vary sentence length naturally. Do not enforce a fixed word count or reading-age score at the cost of correct explanation.
- Define acronyms on first use in each standalone screen or text unit unless research shows the target readers reliably know them. Never assume a term explained on a previous hidden screen is understood.
- Use examples that are realistic, inclusive and relevant to the selected route. Label hypothetical examples as examples. Do not imply an anecdote is representative evidence.
- Preserve uncertainty and distinctions: correlation/causation; observation/intervention; sample/population; estimate/fact; model/reality; forecast/explanation; research training/professional qualification.

## 7. Display is part of the explanation

Good prose can still be hard to understand if it is placed or grouped badly. Every screen must let learners identify the subject, the reason it is shown, the next action and the available depth.

- Use a clear title, short orientation summary, and separate sections for meaning, why it matters on this route, what it builds on, related choices, outputs/limits and resources when present.
- Keep item names, headings and control labels concise; do not append long glossary definitions to a card title or button. Put the first-use explanation in the adjacent body summary. A collapsed card must still explain the term in the visible summary; expanded text may add detail but must not carry the only explanation. If glossary code tracks terms already explained, a title must not mark a term as explained before its body copy is reached.
- Keep distinct items visually distinct. Do not merge a method, tool, certification and output into one “topic” block merely because they are related.
- Keep compulsory learning visually distinct from recommended or optional useful connections. Label the actual implication: required for this route, recommended next, optional, or conditional on a named task.
- Show a meaningful next item after each selection where the graph supports one. State the reason for that next step. Do not force the learner to infer sequence from a list or an unlabeled connector.
- Keep alternatives side by side or clearly grouped, explain when each fits, and do not falsely select all members when one is chosen.
- Explain complementary items as separate choices that can work together; do not present every complement as compulsory.
- Display only valid learning resources, each with its own descriptive action. Omit empty resource sections and inactive placeholder buttons.
- Use semantic headings and labels; visual styling alone must not carry required meaning. Apply WCAG 2.2 for page structure and interaction instructions.
- Support scanning and deeper reading: the short text must orient, while expansion provides technical detail without repeating the preview.

## 8. Source, fidelity and ownership safeguards

Content clarity is not authority to change the learning claim. Before rewriting, inspect the canonical node, route, evidence, source locator, requirement lane, relationship type and context. Record a trace from each substantive student-facing claim to the evidence that supports it.

- Do not add a technical claim, example, prerequisite, output, credential, career promise, legal statement or tool alternative without source support or clearly marked illustrative status.
- If evidence supports only a broad definition, do not make it sound like a tested workflow. If a tool source proves a software feature, do not treat it as evidence that the method is valid.
- Keep domain-, area- and role-owned items at their original level. “Useful for” does not transfer ownership.
- Keep optional and conditional items nonblocking. A clear explanation must not accidentally imply a requirement.
- State limits near the relevant claim; do not hide a crucial caveat at the end of a long page.
- If a source is dated, local, commercial, preliminary, access-limited or based on one role/employer, state that limit when it affects the learner's interpretation.
- Route unresolved factual questions to the responsible evidence owner. Do not silently patch uncertainty with softer wording.

## 9. Review workflow for every enrichment batch

1. **Inventory surfaces.** List all D078 student-visible and proposed copy fields, rendered labels, headings, explanations, relationship text, resources, instructions and report snippets. Include both graph/runtime data and app-generated prose; do not review only item descriptions.
2. **Map content to the route.** For each field, record owner level, learner state/selected route, requirement lane, source IDs, destination/action and where it appears. Mark stale or duplicated strings.
3. **Run the comprehension and terminology audits.** For every text unit, create the term-by-term ledger above; list likely learner questions and answer them in order. Flag undefined technical words (including words inside examples), dense noun phrases, implicit logic, missing reason/next step, misleading certainty, and internal audit language.
4. **Draft for one flow.** Keep technical terms and define them inline. Include a concrete, route-appropriate example, output and caveat where needed. Split into progressive sections only after the core explanation works as continuous prose.
5. **Verify subject-matter accuracy.** Ask a domain reviewer to check every ledger definition, plus the surrounding definitions, assumptions, examples, outputs, dependency claims and limits against sources. Ask the graph/contract owner to verify lanes, ownership, route behavior and conditions.
6. **Edit and inspect display.** Check headings, boundaries, reading order, mobile scanability, repeated copy, resource actions, labels, and screen-reader structure. Read the passage aloud; check that it sounds natural and that pronouns and referents are clear.
7. **Test with readers.** Include first-time learners and adults returning to study, with a mix of prior subject knowledge and reading confidence. Ask them to explain the concept in their own words, identify why it appears, describe what follows, and state what it does not prove. Do not ask only “Is this clear?” Observe where they hesitate or misinterpret.
8. **Revise only the diagnosed gap.** If people misunderstand the technical idea, improve its explanation; if they miss the next step, improve sequence/display; if the claim is wrong, send it to evidence review. Do not solve every problem by adding paragraphs.
9. **Record status.** Track factual review, graph/contract review, editorial review, accessibility/display review and learner comprehension separately. “Passed spelling/readability lint” is not the same as approved content.

## 10. Acceptance checklist

Copy is ready for student-facing review only when all applicable checks pass:

### Accuracy and route fidelity

- [ ] The technical definition matches the cited evidence and does not collapse distinct concepts.
- [ ] Technical terms are retained when they are important learning content and explained at first encounter.
- [ ] A terminology audit ledger covers every technical term and acronym in all visible text, including examples, resources, instructions and reports; no undefined terms remain.
- [ ] The example is realistic, relevant and not presented as proof.
- [ ] Inputs, actions and outputs are accurate where the explanation describes a workflow.
- [ ] Assumptions, uncertainty and important limits are placed near the relevant claim.
- [ ] Requirement, optionality, ownership, area/role context and route condition match the graph/contract.
- [ ] No unverified dependency, career promise, credential implication or universality claim was introduced.

### Reader understanding

- [ ] A first-time learner can say what the concept is without repeating its label.
- [ ] The learner can describe what the method/tool/model actually does.
- [ ] The learner can give a suitable example and identify its output.
- [ ] The learner knows why this item appears in this route and what is suggested next.
- [ ] The learner can distinguish an estimate/pattern/simulation from a fact or causal proof.
- [ ] An adult learner finds the explanation respectful, useful and professionally phrased.

### Display and access

- [ ] The overview states its main point early and uses descriptive headings.
- [ ] Separate concepts and choices are visually and semantically distinguishable.
- [ ] Required, recommended, optional and conditional guidance are not confused.
- [ ] No empty resource area, dead action, internal audit label or concatenated workflow note is shown.
- [ ] Reading order, labels and instructions work for assistive technology and at mobile width.
- [ ] Expanded copy adds depth without repeating the short preview word for word.

## 11. How to use readability tools

Use automated checks to find long sentences, unexplained acronyms, repeated phrases, dense paragraphs, passive constructions and inconsistent terminology. Use these as **flags for human review**, not pass/fail truth. A formula cannot judge whether a social-science claim is accurate, whether an example is relatable, or whether the learner knows what to select next. CDC explicitly describes its Clear Communication Index as a research-based assessment resource and notes that readability formulas alone do not measure audience, purpose or overall comprehension.

Do not target a single reading-age number for all D078 content. The aim is plain-language access to accurate technical meaning, with advanced depth available in the same route. If a score improves only because a key method, qualification or limitation was removed, the edit failed.

## 12. Reusable authoring template

Use this as a planning template, then adapt headings to the screen. Do not show empty sections to students.

```text
Title: [professional term, written clearly]
In one sentence: [what it is + plain-language meaning]
What happens: [question/context, input, operation/reasoning, output]
Example: [small realistic example; state when hypothetical]
Why it appears here: [programme/domain/method/area/task connection]
What you build on: [evidence-backed prerequisite or preparation; say if none is established]
What can come next: [supported next item(s), why, alternatives/complements, skippability]
Limits: [assumptions, data/context limits, uncertainty, ethics, qualification or maturity]
Resources: [valid resource, what it covers, locator, access/action]
Evidence trace: [source IDs + exact locators for substantive claims]
Review: [subject-matter / route-contract / editorial / access / learner review states]
```
